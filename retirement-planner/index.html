FIRE strategies & guidance

FIRE stands for Financial Independence, Retire Early. The core idea: save and invest aggressively until your portfolio can sustainably cover your living expenses, then work becomes optional. Here are the common variants and levers to pull.

Retiring on a minimal, frugal budget (often under ~$40,000/yr). Requires the smallest FIRE number and the fastest path there, but leaves little room for lifestyle inflation, healthcare surprises, or economic downturns. Works best paired with a genuinely low-cost lifestyle and a low-tax, low-cost-of-living state.

Retiring with a much larger portfolio to support an above-average, comfortable lifestyle (often $100,000+/yr in spending). Takes longer to reach and usually requires a high income, but provides far more cushion and flexibility, including for travel, dependents, or a higher cost-of-living area.

You've saved enough that, left alone to compound with zero further contributions, your portfolio will grow to your full FIRE number by traditional retirement age. Once you hit this point you can stop saving for retirement entirely and just cover current living costs — often by working a lower-stress or lower-paying job.

A middle ground: your portfolio covers most of your expenses, and you work part-time (often for supplemental income and employer health insurance) to cover the rest. Reduces the FIRE number needed versus full retirement while still dramatically increasing freedom and flexibility.

The "4% rule" comes from historical studies (notably the Trinity Study) suggesting a portfolio of roughly 60–75% stocks / 25–40% bonds can support a 4% initial withdrawal, adjusted for inflation each year, over a 30-year retirement without running out — in most historical periods. It is a planning heuristic, not a guarantee:

  • Longer retirements (very early retirees with 40–50+ year horizons) often use a more conservative 3–3.5% instead.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk — a market downturn in your first few retirement years — is the biggest threat to a fixed withdrawal plan. Holding 1–2 years of cash/bonds as a buffer, or flexing spending down in bad years, both help.
  • The FIRE number in this tool is calculated as annual spending needed (gross of taxes) ÷ withdrawal rate.
  • Raise your savings rate. Savings rate — not income alone — is the single biggest lever on how fast you reach FIRE; cutting your largest expenses (housing, transportation) usually moves the needle more than cutting small recurring purchases.
  • Max tax-advantaged accounts first. 401(k)/403(b), Traditional or Roth IRA, and HSA (a stealth retirement account — triple tax-advantaged) shelter growth from taxes and often should be filled before taxable brokerage investing.
  • Consider geographic arbitrage. Retiring in — or relocating to — a state with no income tax or generous retirement-income exclusions (see the comparison tool above) can meaningfully lower the gross withdrawal you need, which lowers your FIRE number.
  • Plan the healthcare bridge. Retiring before 65 means bridging to Medicare via ACA marketplace plans, COBRA, a part-time job with benefits (Barista FIRE), or a spouse's plan — budget for this explicitly, it's often the largest underestimated expense.
  • Build a withdrawal order strategy. A common approach: taxable brokerage first, then tax-deferred (Traditional 401k/IRA), then tax-free (Roth) last — to let tax-advantaged growth compound longer, adjusted for your bracket each year.
  • Revisit the plan regularly. Update your inputs above at least yearly, and after any major life change (income, family, relocation) — small changes in return assumptions or spending compound significantly over a multi-decade horizon.
